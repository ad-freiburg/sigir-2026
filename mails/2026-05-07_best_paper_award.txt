## [Topic-Specific Classifiers are Better Relevance Judges than Prompted LLMs](https://openreview.net/forum?id=TYUSDPIqE5)

**Submission 439** | QW: 1.58 | Scores: [1, 2, 2, 2] | Confidences: [5, 4, 4, 4] | Reviewer quality: [3, 2, 3, 3] | SPC quality: 2 | Best paper votes: 3

**Short assessment:** Elegant idea: topic-specific LoRA classifiers to replace LLM-as-a-judge for unjudged documents, achieving >0.94 Spearman correlation across three TREC collections. However, the experimental setup relies on oracle knowledge (known label distributions, global loss weights) unavailable in the real deployment scenario — the gap between reported and real-world performance remains unquantified. A promising research direction with encouraging early evidence, but not yet a validated solution.

**Long assessment:** This paper addresses a genuine and persistent problem in IR evaluation — the unjudged document problem — and proposes a surprisingly simple, principled solution: train topic-specific relevance classifiers via LoRA fine-tuning on a small seed of human judgments, one per topic, rather than relying on LLMs to judge documents at inference time. The core insight is elegant: by deliberately overfitting a monoT5 model to a single assessor's judgments for a single topic, you learn that assessor's idiosyncratic notion of relevance rather than a general-purpose proxy. The reported Spearman's rho of >0.94 with ground-truth system rankings, outperforming zero-shot and few-shot LLM judges, is a strong empirical result across three established benchmarks. The most credible reviewer (Reviewer_rs6x, quality 3, score 1) raised substantive methodological concerns that cannot be easily dismissed: the experimental setup uses stratified sampling per label with a known relevance distribution, which is impossible in the real unjudged-document scenario where the distribution is unknown, and the MSE loss weights are hardcoded to the global dataset imbalance — both of which constitute oracle knowledge that inflates the robustness of the method. The same reviewer correctly notes that Spearman's rho over the full run pool is insensitive to the top ranks where it matters most for test collection reuse, and that binarizing graded TREC-DL labels makes the NDCG ground truth misaligned with official results. Reviewers_rTs9 and PUDp (both quality 3) add a related and important concern: the classifiers are fitted to pools built by lexical systems, and their ability to fairly judge documents retrieved by neural rankers operating outside the original pool is untested, which partially undercuts the paper's central promise of extending older collections to modern systems. The SPC (quality 2) acknowledges the practical applicability concern but waves away the evaluation concerns as camera-ready items without explaining why oracle bias is not a showstopper — and the meta-review's opening "Great idea!" signals enthusiasm rather than rigorous adjudication. The paper's contribution is real: topic-specific LoRA classifiers are a clever, lightweight counter to the circularity of LLM-as-a-judge, and the framing is sharp and well-written. But the gap between the idealized experimental conditions and the real-world unjudged-document scenario is large enough, and the evaluation narrow enough (Spearman only, binarized labels, no significance preservation), that this paper is more of a promising research direction with encouraging early evidence than a fully validated solution ready to be adopted as the new standard.

## [Consensus-Anchored Expansion for Noise-Resilient Re-ranking](https://openreview.net/forum?id=Nk118oAX2L)

**Submission 567** | QW: 1.42 | Scores: [1, 2, 1, 1] | Confidences: [4, 4, 5, 3] | Reviewer quality: [3, 4, 3, 3] | SPC quality: 3 | Best paper votes: 0

**Short assessment:** Reframes sparse-dense retriever agreement as a reliability signal for PRF seed selection — a genuinely novel conceptual move. The 97% relevant-passage concentration in the consensus region is a striking diagnostic finding, and the training-free pipeline delivers consistent 3.4-5.7% nDCG gains. Missing QE baselines (RM3, ColBERT-PRF) and evaluation on only two TREC-DL benchmarks leave generality undemonstrated. A clean, well-motivated paper whose scope is narrower than its insight deserves.

**Long assessment:** AgreRank addresses a genuine and persistent problem in hybrid retrieval: both sparse and dense retrievers have systematic failure modes, and their failures are complementary — yet most existing work fuses their scores rather than exploiting the structure of their agreement. The paper's central insight, that the consensus region (where both retrievers score highly) concentrates 97% of relevant passages with an order-of-magnitude precision lift, is empirically striking and motivates the 'purify-then-expand' pipeline in a principled way. The training-free, parameter-free design is a real strength: the gains of 3.4–5.7% nDCG@10 over strong Dense+CE and RRF+CE baselines using only a lightweight 33M cross-encoder are meaningful in a field where improvements of this scale typically require additional parameters or supervision. Reviewer_Pg8v (quality 4), the most substantive reviewer, independently and correctly framed the novelty as a conceptual reframing of retriever agreement as a reliability signal rather than as a single algorithmic innovation — this is an honest and credible assessment of where the contribution lies. The most credible weakness, raised by Reviewer_Pg8v (quality 4) and Reviewer_p5tf (quality 3, confidence 5), is the narrow evaluation scope: only TREC-DL 2021/22, only BM25 as the sparse signal, and no comparison to standard query expansion baselines such as RM3, ColBERT-PRF, or ANCE-PRF. Without these, it is difficult to place AgreRank's gains in the broader QE literature, and its behavior on more diverse query sets (e.g., BEIR) remains unknown. The SPC meta-review, rated quality 3, acknowledges this limitation but does not weigh it decisively, and the AC decision is a rubber stamp adding no independent signal. AgreRank is a clean, well-motivated, and practically useful paper with a genuinely interesting diagnostic contribution, but its narrow experimental scope and absence of QE baselines leave the committee without the evidence needed to confidently call it the best work at the conference.

## [FedMosaic: Federated Retrieval-Augmented Generation via Parametric Adapters](https://openreview.net/forum?id=mys9K6C4Yd)

**Submission 576** | QW: 1.25 | Scores: [2, 1, 1, 1] | Confidences: [4, 3, 4, 5] | Reviewer quality: [3, 3, 2, 4] | SPC quality: 3 | Best paper votes: 0

**Short assessment:** Tackles privacy-preserving federated RAG with a coherent two-part design: multi-document LoRA adapters with binary masks, plus conflict-aware greedy aggregation backed by an NP-hardness proof. Strong empirical gains (10.9% accuracy, 78-91% storage/communication reduction). The mask-overlap conflict proxy is unvalidated, the privacy threat model incomplete, and offline training cost unanalyzed. A solid, timely contribution whose claims outrun its validation in key places.

**Long assessment:** FedMosaic tackles a genuine and increasingly important problem: how to do retrieval-augmented generation when documents cannot leave their silos — a strict locality constraint that rules out standard in-context RAG. The paper's answer is elegant in structure: encode documents into lightweight LoRA adapters (with document-specific binary masks to preserve specificity within shared cluster-level adapters), then at inference time retrieve and selectively aggregate only non-conflicting, relevance-aligned adapters. The NP-hardness proof for conflict-aware selection and the accompanying greedy heuristic give the work theoretical grounding that distinguishes it from purely engineering-driven system papers. The empirical results are strong on their face — 10.9% average accuracy gain over state-of-the-art, 78–86% storage reduction, 91% communication reduction — and the most credible reviewer (Reviewer_9sRH, quality 4, confidence 5) confirmed these as genuine contributions while categorizing their concerns as minor polish rather than core technical problems. The most technically engaged critical reviewer (Reviewer_8cVi, quality 3, confidence 3) raised real and unresolved concerns: the novelty positioning is somewhat overstated (parametric RAG via adapters is prior work; the contribution is the federated/locality adaptation plus the clustering and conflict-aware aggregation), the use of mask overlap as a conflict proxy lacks direct empirical validation, the greedy selection lacks robustness guarantees, and the privacy threat model is incomplete relative to the paper's claims. These are genuine paper weaknesses, not process noise. The offline training cost — running LLM forward/backward passes for every document to learn binary masks — is a practical scalability concern that the paper does not analyze empirically, a gap Reviewer_TyuR (quality 3) articulated clearly in discussion. The problem is important and timely, the design is cohesive and novel in its federated setting, and the results are compelling, but the paper's claims outrun its validation in key places — particularly around the conflict proxy, the privacy model, and offline efficiency — making it a solid contribution that stops short of the field-defining impact a best-paper award should recognize.

## [Exploration-and-Thinking: Agentic Reasoning over Knowledge Graphs via an LLM-RL Synergized Framework](https://openreview.net/forum?id=n8r4pfg5vJ)

**Submission 1268** | QW: 1.17 | Scores: [1, 1, 2, 1] | Confidences: [3, 4, 4, 4] | Reviewer quality: [3, 2, 3, 3] | SPC quality: 2 | Best paper votes: 0

**Short assessment:** Elegantly decouples RL-based KG path exploration from LLM semantic validation, reducing LLM calls from O(hops) to O(k) while an 8B model beats GPT-4o on three KGQA benchmarks. The efficiency insight is real and the empirical results are strong. But the 'plug-and-play' claim is overstated — the GNN/RL policy is tightly coupled to the training KG's schema, and evaluation is confined to Freebase. A well-executed paper whose transferability claims outrun its evidence.

**Long assessment:** EAT addresses a genuine and timely pain point in LLM-KG integration: the prohibitive cost and semantic drift that arise when LLM inference is tightly coupled to KG traversal at every hop. The core design — decoupling RL-based path exploration (via a lightweight GNN agent) from LLM-based semantic validation, and using LLM feedback to shape RL rewards against spurious paths — is both conceptually clean and practically motivated. The most technically substantive reviewer (Reviewer_veio, quality 3) raised a precise and important concern: the GNN representations and RL action space are tightly bound to the target KG's relation vocabulary and topology, which directly undermines the paper's 'plug-and-play' and 'modular' claims. Reviewer_WrHk (quality 3) echoed this with the zero-shot transfer question. These are real limitations of the paper: if the exploration policy must be retrained for each new KG, the framework is less general than advertised, and the evaluation restricted to Freebase-based benchmarks leaves the generalization question unanswered. That said, the empirical results are strong — SOTA across WebQSP, CWQ, and GrailQA, outperforming GPT-4o with an 8B backbone, with thorough ablations, efficiency analysis, and robustness tests. The efficiency gain (O(k) LLM calls rather than linear) is a meaningful practical contribution. The SPC meta-review and AC decision add no independent signal, and three of four reviewers rated the paper as weak reject, so the positive reception reflects a generous bar rather than reviewer enthusiasm. EAT is a solid, well-executed paper with a clean idea and convincing empirics, but its transferability claims outrun its evidence, and it falls short of the field-advancing originality that best-paper recognition demands.

## [Formalized Information Needs Improve Large-Language-Model Relevance Judgments](https://openreview.net/forum?id=X2l4FJTQJ0)

**Submission 1682** | QW: 1.08 | Scores: [0, 1, 0, 2] | Confidences: [4, 4, 3, 5] | Reviewer quality: [3, 3, 3, 5] | SPC quality: 3 | Best paper votes: 1

**Short assessment:** Demonstrates that structured topic formalization substantially reduces LLM positivity bias in relevance assessment — a clear, actionable finding validated across three benchmarks, multiple LLM families, and seven prompt variants, with 50k topics and 1.7M judgments released. The quality-5 reviewer called it 'immediate practical benefit to a large audience.' The circularity concern (same model family for generation and judging) is real but bounded. Solid, useful work the community will cite and apply, but empirical validation rather than conceptual surprise.

**Long assessment:** This paper addresses a genuinely important and timely question: when LLMs are used as relevance assessors, should they be given only a raw query or a fully formalized information need (title + description + narrative in TREC style)? The answer the paper delivers is clear and empirically well-supported — formalized topics reduce positivity bias, improve agreement with human judgments, and produce more reliable benchmarks — and the practical implications for the community conducting LLM-based evaluations are immediate and concrete. The strongest endorsement comes from Reviewer_GkAb (quality 5, confidence 5), who calls it 'commendable work that should have immediate practical benefit to a large audience' and engages with the paper's formal framework at a technical level, noting that the leave-one-out tests wisely guard against over-fitting. The paper's contribution is more empirical than methodological: it validates a reasonable hypothesis with careful, multi-dataset, multi-model experiments covering Robust04, DL19, and DL20, with seven prompt variants and four LLM families, and releases 50k generated topics and 1.7M relevance judgments as a resource. The main genuine weaknesses — raised by reviewers of middling quality (3/5) — are the circularity concern (using the same model family for both topic generation and judging, which the paper does not fully confront) and the absence of a direct comparison between LLM-generated and human-written TREC topics on Robust04 where human narratives already exist. These are real gaps rather than nitpicks, but they are scope limitations rather than methodological flaws that undermine the core claim. The paper does not advance fundamental theory, and its novelty is incremental — it applies the well-known insight that 'instructions to assessors matter' to the LLM-as-judge setting — but it does so rigorously and with enough practical consequence to matter. As a best-paper candidate, this is solid, useful work that the community will cite and apply, but it lacks the conceptual surprise or methodological leap that distinguishes a landmark paper from a very good one.

## [Following the Eye-Tracking Evidence: Established Web-Search Assumptions Fail in Carousel Interfaces](https://openreview.net/forum?id=ZlSea9DEvC)

**Submission 781** | QW: 1.08 | Scores: [1, 1, 1, 1] | Confidences: [4, 4, 3, 5] | Reviewer quality: [3, 4, 2, 4] | SPC quality: 2 | Best paper votes: 0

**Short assessment:** Uses eye-tracking data to challenge three assumptions (F-pattern, examination hypothesis, heading salience) borrowed uncritically from web search into carousel interfaces — a genuine empirical gap. The L-pattern finding on clicks is specific and actionable. Both quality-4 reviewers independently flagged the central smoothing procedure as insufficiently justified, which matters because key conclusions rest on smoothed data. A timely corrective study whose methodological foundation needs strengthening.

**Long assessment:** This paper addresses a genuine blind spot in information retrieval research: carousel interfaces have been ubiquitous in streaming and e-commerce for years, yet the field has been borrowing behavioral assumptions (F-pattern, examination hypothesis) directly from single-list web search without empirical verification. The contribution is empirical — using the RecGaze eye-tracking dataset to test these assumptions — and the findings are consequential: the F-pattern does not generalize horizontally, the examination hypothesis fails in this setting, and users largely bypass carousel headings. The 'L-pattern' observation (examination following a click) is a specific, memorable contribution with direct implications for click model design. The strongest reviewers (Mjfm at quality 4, Wq7r at quality 4) both raised the same substantive methodological concern: the paper's core results in sections 5.2 and 6 depend heavily on a gradient-boosted smoothing procedure for sparse click data, and the paper does not provide sufficient justification that this smoothing is neutral with respect to the conclusions — as Mjfm noted in a well-reasoned thought experiment, under- or over-smoothing could support opposite findings. This is a real weakness of the paper, not the process. Reviewer_Wq7r also correctly observed that grounding the hypotheses only in blue-link search weakens the framing, since visually rich interfaces (federated search, image search) have already been shown to alter user behavior, making some carousel-specific deviations less surprising than presented. The single-dataset limitation is universally acknowledged and constrains generalizability, though the scarcity of public eye-tracking data on carousel UIs is a field-wide constraint rather than an authorial choice. The SPC meta-review is low quality (rating 2) — it summarizes reviewer concerns without weighing them — so it adds no independent signal about the paper's merit. This is a solid, timely empirical paper that points the field toward a genuine calibration problem in carousel evaluation, but the unresolved questions around smoothing validity prevent it from being a clear best-paper candidate.

## [Debiased Recommendation Beyond the Positive Propensity Assumption](https://openreview.net/forum?id=EyGmyMOZ3d)

**Submission 447** | QW: 1.0 | Scores: [1, 2, 1, 1] | Confidences: [4, 4, 4, 3] | Reviewer quality: [2, 2, 4, 2] | SPC quality: 2 | Best paper votes: 0

**Short assessment:** Proves that IPS/DR estimators are biased under zero-propensity conditions created by hard filtering rules, then proposes a principled Lipschitz-bounded minimax framework — theory that explains why existing methods structurally fail, not just empirically underperform. Validated with four offline datasets and a production A/B test on a food-delivery platform. The heuristic zero-propensity identification and public-dataset simulation are real but bounded limitations. A paper the IR community will cite when it finally takes selection bias in production recommenders seriously.

**Long assessment:** This paper identifies a real and underappreciated gap in the debiased recommendation literature: standard IPS and DR estimators implicitly assume every user-item pair has nonzero propensity, yet hard filtering rules (geo-blocking, eligibility constraints) routinely violate this, and no prior work has formally addressed the consequences. The theoretical contribution is solid — the paper proves bias of IPS/DR under zero-propensity conditions and derives a worst-case upper bound for the extrapolation region under a Lipschitz continuity assumption, unifying both regions in a three-step pipeline. The strongest reviewer (Reviewer_VwPC, quality 4) raised technically grounded concerns: the heuristic identification of extrapolation samples conflates truly impossible pairs with merely rare ones, public-dataset simulation via isolation-forest removal may not match real rule-based blocking, and the extrema-based bounds could be sensitive to noisy neighbors — all legitimate weaknesses that the paper does not fully resolve. However, the same reviewer ultimately moved to acceptance, judging the concerns minor relative to the contribution. The AC (quality 4), who read the paper independently, called out the key insight clearly: this paper attacks a fundamental assumption rather than making a surface-level improvement, which is the right lens for evaluating its significance. The online A/B test on a production food-delivery system provides rare real-world validation. The weaknesses are real but bounded: the Lipschitz assumption is acknowledged as a limitation, the computational overhead of three sequential models is modest in practice, and the heuristic identification step is a practical necessity given the zero-propensity problem is inherently unidentified from data alone. As a best-paper candidate, this paper does not have the sweep or surprise of a landmark result, but it makes a genuinely principled contribution to a practical problem that affects every large-scale recommender system with hard filtering — solid, honest, and backed by production evidence, yet not quite the kind of work that redefines a field.

## [Verbalizing LightGCN: Direct Learning of Textual Representations from User-Item Interaction Graph via LLMs](https://openreview.net/forum?id=oVCw9kB5CM)

**Submission 897** | QW: 1.0 | Scores: [1, 1, 1, 1] | Confidences: [4, 4, 4, 3] | Reviewer quality: [3, 4, 2, 3] | SPC quality: 3 | Best paper votes: 0

**Short assessment:** Replaces every numerical operation in LightGCN with a language-native equivalent — aggregation via Chain-of-Thought, optimization via Textual Gradient Descent — resulting in a fully text-profile-based recommender. The conceptual completeness is genuinely novel, not just another LLM-augmented CF paper. But evaluation is limited to tiny subsets (33k interactions max), variance is unreported, and the scalability question is unanswered. A stimulating proof of concept whose empirical validation is too constrained for its ambitions.

**Long assessment:** VerbaLightGCN pursues a genuinely novel conceptual move: instead of using LLMs as feature extractors bolted onto a traditional CF backbone, it replaces every numerical operation in LightGCN — aggregation, prediction, and optimization — with an equivalent natural-language operation performed by an LLM. The result is a system where users and items are represented as evolving text profiles, graph aggregation becomes Chain-of-Thought prompting over a node's neighborhood, and backpropagation is replaced by a Textual Gradient Descent mechanism in which the LLM reads its own errors and updates profiles accordingly. This is conceptually clean and genuinely distinct from the dominant paradigm of LLM-augmented CF. The AC's independent summary is the most credible endorsement of the paper's framing, and Reviewer_Dscr (quality 4), who engaged substantively, found the parallel construction of VerbaLightGCN against LightGCN components to be clear and interesting while maintaining a positive score. However, the paper's weaknesses are real and non-trivial. The most substantive critical reviewer (KtXk, quality 3) correctly identified that the approach is evaluated only on very small subsets — 33k interactions in the largest experiment — which raises serious questions about whether the Chain-of-Thought aggregation degrades gracefully as interaction graphs grow; this scalability gap is not just an engineering detail but touches the claim that the framework genuinely learns from interaction structure. The absence of variance reporting across runs is a methodological gap given known LLM instability under prompt and positional variation. The restriction to only strong positive feedback (ratings > 3) narrows the evaluation scope without sufficient justification. The strongest reviewer (Dscr) also flagged missing related work on natural-language user profiles — a line of work directly relevant to the paper's interpretability claims — and raised legitimate concerns about the evaluation of the explanation generation component. Taken together, VerbaLightGCN is a creative proof-of-concept that opens a genuinely new direction in recommendation, but its empirical validation is too constrained and its positioning relative to related work too incomplete for it to rank among the strongest candidates for best paper.

## [Why Advanced Encoders Lag on Sparse Retrieval? The Answer and an Approach to Bridging Vocabulary Gaps](https://openreview.net/forum?id=pFziI9lb0T)

**Submission 86** | QW: 0.83 | Scores: [1, 0, 1, 1] | Confidences: [4, 4, 5, 4] | Reviewer quality: [4, 3, 3, 3] | SPC quality: 4 | Best paper votes: 0

**Short assessment:** Diagnoses why modern encoders lag behind BERT-base in sparse retrieval — vocabulary normalization, not architecture — and delivers a cheap fix (500 MLM steps) that rescues RoBERTa-large from 1.4 to 51.3 nDCG. The diagnostic is clean, the practical payoff immediate. The unjustified APC constant c=5 and exclusive use of bert-base-uncased as target vocabulary leave generality partially unvalidated. A strong paper with a fixable gap between its ambitions and its evidence.

**Long assessment:** This paper solves a concrete, widely-felt puzzle in the IR community: why do modern pre-trained encoders like ModernBERT fall behind aging BERT-base in learned sparse retrieval? The answer — vocabulary normalization, not architecture — is both surprising and actionable. The diagnostic is clean: the bert-base-cased control experiment isolates normalization as the variable with unusual precision, and the VT remedy is impressively cheap (500 MLM steps to near-convergence). The headline results are genuinely compelling: ModernBERT jumps from 47.7 to 52.4 nDCG on BEIR, and RoBERTa-large is resurrected from effectively non-functional (1.4) to competitive (51.3). The best-quality reviewer (quality 4, confidence 4) found the RoBERTa-large result 'highly compelling' and praised the method's practical significance; two other reviewers (quality 3, confidence 4-5) agreed the contribution is useful despite reservations. The most substantive weaknesses — flagged by all reviewers and confirmed by the SPC — are real: the APC constant c=5 is a magic number with no sensitivity analysis, every open-domain experiment locks in bert-base-uncased as the target vocabulary with no controlled alternative, statistical significance is absent for the key 0.4-point SOTA claim, and Section 3's formal machinery adds density without proportionate insight. The SPC (quality 4) addressed these clearly and rejected the 'patch-not-root-cause' objection with a sensible argument about the realistic landscape of pre-trained models. The paper's contribution is solid and immediately practical, but the unvalidated APC parameter and the single-vocabulary fixation leave a nagging question about how far VT generalizes beyond the specific configuration tested — concerns too real to dismiss and too fixable to treat as fatal.
